"Assistant" covers a lot of ground. Two businesses can hire an assistant and end up with completely different roles, one clearing a backlog of admin and the other quietly running a leader's week.

The two most common versions are the **virtual assistant** and the **executive assistant**. They overlap, but they solve different problems. Picking the wrong one usually means either underusing a skilled person or expecting judgment the role wasn't set up for.

## The core difference

The simplest way to think about it:

- A **virtual assistant owns tasks.** They take defined, recurring work off your team's plate and do it reliably.
- An **executive assistant owns a person's time.** They protect one leader's priorities, manage their communication, and make sure nothing important slips.

Both can work remotely. Both handle email and calendars. The difference is in scope, context, and trust.

## What a virtual assistant typically handles

Virtual assistants are strongest with work that follows a pattern:

- Inbox triage and routine replies
- Scheduling and calendar upkeep
- Data entry, CRM updates, and record keeping
- Document formatting and file organization
- Research and structured summaries
- Recurring weekly and monthly admin

A virtual assistant often supports several people or a whole team, working from shared checklists and clear instructions. If you're unsure what that looks like day to day, we cover it in [what a virtual assistant actually handles](/blog/what-does-a-virtual-assistant-handle).

## What an executive assistant typically handles

Executive assistants work closer to one leader and need more context to do the job well:

- Managing the leader's calendar around real priorities, not just availability
- Screening and drafting communication on the leader's behalf
- Preparing briefings, agendas, and follow-ups for important meetings
- Coordinating travel, events, and personal logistics
- Tracking commitments the leader has made and making sure they're kept
- Acting as a point of contact for clients, partners, or the team

The best executive assistants anticipate. They notice the conflict before it lands on your calendar and draft the reply before you ask.

## Five questions to help you decide

### 1. Are you trying to clear tasks or protect a person?

If the pain is a pile of recurring work, start with a virtual assistant. If the pain is one leader's week being consumed by coordination, look at an executive assistant.

### 2. How much context does the work need?

Work you can explain in a checklist suits a virtual assistant. Work that depends on knowing your relationships, priorities, and preferences suits an executive assistant.

### 3. Who needs support?

A team with shared admin needs usually benefits from a virtual assistant. A founder or executive whose time is the bottleneck usually benefits from an executive assistant.

### 4. How much discretion is involved?

Executive assistants often handle confidential information and speak on a leader's behalf. That requires a higher level of trust, built deliberately over time.

### 5. How much time can you invest in the handoff?

Both roles need onboarding. An executive assistant needs more of it, because learning how you think takes longer than learning a process.

## A common path: start with one, grow into the other

Many founders start with a virtual assistant to take recurring admin off their plate. As trust builds and the assistant learns the business, the role naturally expands into calendar ownership, meeting prep, and communication. That's the move toward executive support.

Others start with an executive assistant because their own time is the constraint, then add a virtual assistant later for team-wide admin.

Either path works. What matters is being clear about the job you're hiring for today.

## Signs you've outgrown a virtual assistant setup

- You're spending time explaining context before every task.
- Important emails wait because your assistant can't judge what's urgent to you.
- Your calendar is technically managed but doesn't reflect your priorities.
- You want someone to represent you, not just complete tasks.
