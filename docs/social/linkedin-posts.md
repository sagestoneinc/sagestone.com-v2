## 1. What does a virtual assistant actually handle?

**Post:**

"Get a VA" is common advice for busy founders.

What's less common is an answer to the next question: *what exactly would they do?*

Here's the simple test I use:

If a task happens every week, can be written as a checklist, and doesn't need your personal judgment each time, someone else can own it.

That usually covers:
→ Inbox triage and routine replies
→ Scheduling and meeting prep
→ CRM updates and data entry
→ Invoice follow-ups
→ Research and summaries

What should stay with you:
→ Decisions that set direction
→ Relationships only you can hold
→ Judgment calls with no pattern yet

The mistake I see most: handing over "everything" instead of a clear list. Start with 3 tasks. Document them. Expand from there.

## 2. Virtual assistant vs. executive assistant

**Post:**

Two founders both "hire an assistant."

One gets their admin backlog cleared.
The other gets their week back.

Same title. Very different jobs.

→ A virtual assistant owns tasks: recurring, defined work done reliably.
→ An executive assistant owns a person's time: priorities, communication, and making sure nothing important slips.

Quick way to tell which you need:

Is the pain a pile of recurring work? Start with a VA.
Is the pain one leader's calendar and inbox? Look at an EA.

Many founders start with a VA and grow into executive support as trust builds. Both paths work. What matters is being clear about the job you're hiring for today.

## 3. When should a founder hire a virtual assistant?

**Post:**

Most founders hire help later than they should.

Doing everything yourself feels efficient early on. Then the business grows, and those same habits become the bottleneck.

7 signs it's time:

1. You do the same tasks every week
2. Strategy only happens after hours
3. Follow-ups are slipping
4. You answer the same questions repeatedly
5. Your calendar runs your day
6. Growth waits on your availability
7. You can't take a day off without things stalling

"I don't have time to train someone" is the most common objection.

But onboarding a few recurring tasks takes hours once. Those tasks then leave your plate every week, for good.

Start small: 3 to 5 recurring tasks, simply documented.

## 4. In-house vs. outsourced customer support

**Post:**

Every growing business hits the same wall:

Customer questions arrive faster than the team can answer them.

Then comes the question: build support in-house, or outsource it?

In-house gives you direct control and deep product knowledge, but it takes time to hire, train, and manage.

Outsourcing gives you speed to coverage and structure you don't have to build, but it needs a clear handoff.

Most teams I work with land on a hybrid:
→ Outsourced tier-one support for high-volume, well-documented questions
→ Clear escalation rules for anything sensitive
→ An internal owner who reviews trends and feeds issues back to product

The deciding question is often this: who would manage an in-house hire? If the honest answer is "the founder, in spare time," outsourcing the structure matters as much as the work.

## 5. Customer service QA checklist

**Post:**

Fast replies are half of good customer support.

The other half is consistency: every customer getting an accurate, on-brand answer, no matter who replied or how busy the week was.

That's what a QA checklist is for. Mine covers 5 areas:

✅ Accuracy: correct, current, and checked, not assumed
✅ Resolution: the actual question answered, with a clear next step
✅ Tone: sounds like your brand, and acknowledges frustration
✅ Clarity: easy to scan, no internal jargon
✅ Process: tagged right, notes left, escalated when needed

The real value of QA isn't catching mistakes.

It's spotting the gaps behind them: the outdated saved reply, the unclear policy, the missing escalation rule. Fix those once in your documentation instead of correcting the same thing ticket by ticket.

## 6. How to onboard a remote virtual assistant

**Post:**

Most VA relationships are decided in the first 30 days.

Rushed onboarding: the assistant guesses, the founder gets frustrated, and everyone decides "delegation doesn't work for us."

Deliberate onboarding: the same person becomes someone you can't imagine working without.

The 30-day plan I recommend:

Before day 1 → a short task list, the right tool access, and rough documentation
Week 1 → context and shadowing, plus a daily 15-minute check-in
Week 2 → supervised ownership: they do it, you review, and you update the docs together
Weeks 3–4 → spot-checks instead of full review, then add the next responsibilities

The habit that matters most: fast, specific feedback.

"This isn't quite right" slows everything down.
"Replies to existing clients should use their first name and feel warmer" compounds.

## 7. How to document repetitive processes

**Post:**

Most delegation fails for one quiet reason:

The work was never written down.

The fastest SOP method I know:

1. Pick a process that's frequent, rules-based, and painful
2. Screen-record yourself doing it, narrating the why
3. Turn the recording into numbered steps: purpose, trigger, owner, tools, steps, exceptions, and what "done" means
4. Have someone else follow it exactly. Every question they ask is a gap
5. Store it in one place, linked from the tools where the work happens
6. Update it the moment something changes

A 10-minute recording captures more than an hour of trying to remember the steps.

SOPs don't need to be long or formal. They need to be clear enough that someone else can do the work the way you would.

📅 Scheduling

🤝 Client onboarding steps
📇 CRM updates
🧾 Invoice prep and follow-up
🗂 Expense and receipt tracking
📦 Vendor coordination
📘 Writing SOPs
📊 Weekly reporting

How to choose where to start: score each task 1 to 3 for time spent and energy drained. Start with the highest scores that can be written as steps.

What stays with you: strategy, key relationships, hiring, and the judgment calls that don't have a pattern yet.

Pick 3. Document them. Hand them over properly.

## 9. Real estate virtual assistants: a practical guide

**Post:**

Real estate runs on responsiveness.

Leads expect quick replies. Transactions have hard deadlines. Clients want updates at every step.

And the admin behind all of it competes with the work that actually closes deals.

What a real estate virtual assistant can take on:
→ Transaction coordination: dates, documents, and updates from contract to close
→ CRM upkeep: lead entry, tagging, pipeline updates, and follow-up reminders
→ Listing admin: descriptions, photos, and portal updates
→ Scheduling: showings, inspections, and appraisals
→ Property management support: tenant inquiries, maintenance logs, and owner reports

Where to start: pick 2 or 3 areas, write down your contract-to-close checklist, and agree on clear rules for what needs your approval.
